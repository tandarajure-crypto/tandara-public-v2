THE THIRD BRANCH OF THE FIRST TANDARA FAMILY

3.0. Ante Tandara – Ivanov, son of Ivan (1730–)

Ante, son of Ivan, married Matija, née Brnas (D.U.). They had six children: sons Ante (1769–1849), Filip (D.U.) and Pavle (1777–), and daughters Jaka (1771–1837), Luca (D.U.) and Ruža (D.U.).

Family member codes: 3.0-SP-1 Matija née Brnas 3.1 Ante 3.2 Filip 3.0-S1 Pavle 3.0-K1 Jaka 3.0-K2 Luca 3.0-K3 Ruža

Ante, son of Ivan, was the third son of our first common ancestor, Ivan, who was born around 1695, and was a few years younger than his brother Petar. He had three sons—Ante, Filip and Pavle—and three daughters—Jaka, Luca and Ruža.

Ante, son of Ante, and Filip, son of Ante, continued the family line, while Pavle’s fate is unknown. Apart from his birth record, no further information about him has been found, suggesting that he probably died very young or left Ričice before leaving any further trace in the parish registers.

The eldest son, Ante, moved with his family to Zavelim around 1819, where his branch developed. Filip remained in Ričice, where his line took its clearest shape.

Ante’s line remained firmly connected to Ričice and Zavelim, where it developed most clearly during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. This family nucleus produced the largest number of people bearing the Tandara surname, so Ante’s line is regarded as the most extensive among Ivan’s descendants.

In later generations, members of this branch moved to other parts of Croatia and then to European Union countries, Canada, the United States and Australia, extending the family line far beyond its original homeland.

FIRST SUB-BRANCH OF ANTE ANTIN

3.1. Ante, son of Ante (1769–1849)

As the eldest son of Ante, son of Ivan, Ante married Matija, née Jukić, in 1801. They had three sons—Lovro, Mijo and Petar (1821–)—and six daughters— Pera (1802–), Ruža (1804–), Antica (1807–), Iva (1808–), a second daughter named Antica (1814–), and Marija (1823–). Their descendants later spread throughout Croatia and Europe, as well as to the United States and Canada.

Josip Jurin was married to Anica, née Dragun (1911–1989). They had eight children: sons Ivan (1933–2025), Milan (1942–2003) and Martin (1946–1947), and daughters Slava (1935–1977), Mara (1936–2012), Neda (1939–2025), Matija (1940–) and Zlata (1944–1945).

Family member codes: 3.1.1.2.4.1-SP-1 Anica Dragun 3.1.1.2.4.1.1 Ivan 3.1.1.2.4.1.2 Milan 3.1.1.2.4.1-S1 Martin 3.1.1.2.4.1-K1 Slava 3.1.1.2.4.1-K2 Mara 3.1.1.2.4.1-K3 Neda 3.1.1.2.4.1-K4 Matija 3.1.1.2.4.1-K5 Zlata

Josip Jurin was tragically killed in 1946 in circumstances that have never been officially clarified. According to eyewitness Marijan Budimir (Mijan), who gave his account to Josip’s wife Anica, née Dragun, shortly before his own death, Josip was killed by his next-door neighbour acting at the urging and direction of an older brother. According to that testimony, the motive was material gain: Josip worked as a trader in the cooperative and had access to goods and supplies that were extremely valuable in the post-war period.

In the years after the war, an account spread publicly that Josip had been killed by retreating “kamišari”. That version was never confirmed in court, and the entire case remained uninvestigated and unprosecuted because of post-war conditions, unsettled social relations and deliberate manipulation of information. The event is presented here solely as family testimony and the historical experience of his descendants, not as a fact established by a court.

Josip’s death and the long uncertainty surrounding its true circumstances profoundly marked the lives of his wife, children and later generations. It was especially difficult to accept that the man who, according to the family testimony, organised the killing later helped Josip’s family in various ways. This created a deep inner struggle for Josip’s son Ivan—my father—who, until his death in 2025, could not bring himself to believe the eyewitness testimony.

Author’s Note

Because of contradictory public accounts, our family was subjected for decades to labels that did not reflect what we learned from the eyewitness account and from our own family testimony. The claim that Josip had been a victim of the “kamišari” was used by representatives of the authorities at the time as grounds for erecting a monument to him and including his death in official commemorations of the former state.

I include this note to distinguish official and subsequently shaped folk accounts from the eyewitness testimony and family memory. Its purpose is not to accuse descendants today or to create hostility. Relations between the families are now normal and friendly, based on mutual respect and understanding.

This record therefore represents family memory of an event whose circumstances were interpreted in different ways over the decades. It is preserved as a memory carried by Josip’s descendants.

Recording this family testimony does not seek condemnation or revive old divisions. Its purpose is to honour the generation that endured these events and to preserve the family’s account for later generations.

She joined Nova TV's news department in 2005 and covers political affairs, including the Office of the President of the Republic, the Government and parliamentary parties.

She graduated from the Faculty of Political Science at the University of Zagreb and completed a new-media workshop at the University of Missouri through a programme organised by the United States Embassy.

She has reported on presidential, parliamentary and local elections, Croatia's accession to the European Union and official visits by Croatian state leaders. She has also reported from the war zone in Afghanistan, while her public work includes political interviews, reports, live coverage and election programmes.
