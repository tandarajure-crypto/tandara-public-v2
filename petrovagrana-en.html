THE SECOND BRANCH OF THE FIRST TANDARA FAMILY

2.0. Petar Tandara – Ivanov (1734.–1817.)

Petar Tandara was Ivan’s second son. He died in 1817 at the age of eighty-three, so it is estimated that he was born around 1734. Petar was married to Jela, née Budić. They had seven sons—Grgo, Jakov (1773–), Martin, Marijan (1781–), Mijo, Tadija and Marko (1787–)—and two daughters, Manda (1777–) and Jela (–1824). The sons Jakov (1773–), Marijan (1781–), Tadija and Marko (1787–) do not appear in the 1806 Register of Souls, and Tadija is also absent from the 1819 register. It may therefore be assumed that they were no longer alive or that one of them had moved away from Ričice.

THE BEGINNING OF MIGRATION BEYOND RIČICE

In 1819, the family of Martin Tandara, Lovro’s grandfather, was living in Zavelim. Martin’s sons Ivan, Mijo and Grgo were listed there. When the Austrian cadastral survey was conducted in 1835, Martin Tandara, son of the late Petar, was recorded as a property owner in Ričice. At that time, most clans from Ričice owned property in Ričice, Zavelim, Podbila and Vir. These villages belonged to the same parish in Ričice. Many families within the same clan moved from one locality to another, even within Ričice itself. The population was growing rapidly and new living space was needed. In this context, it is understandable that a family, or part of a family, could be recorded in Ričice in one register and in Zavelim in the next, or vice versa. It is known with certainty, however, that Grgo returned with his children from Zavelim to Ričice, where he continued living with his family.

2.2. Martin Petrov (1775.–1844.)

Martin, Petar’s third son, married Ruža, née Lekić. Between the 1806 and 1819 censuses, Martin moved with his family from Ričice to Zavelim together with Ante, the son of his paternal uncle. They had six sons—Ivan, Jure (1811–), Mijo, Grgo, Filip (1821–) and Josip (1824–)—and two daughters, Matija and Ana (1828–). No records of Jure, Filip or Josip have been found apart from their baptismal entries, so it is not known whether they died young or moved away. Mijo’s sons left no male descendants, so the family line was continued only by Martin’s sons Ivan and Grgo.

2.2.3.1.1.3.1. Ante Bruno Matin (1937.–1989.)

Dr. Ante Bruno Tandara belonged to a generation of Croatian intellectuals who, long before the decisive historical moment, clearly articulated the need for a free, independent and equal Croatian state. His social and political engagement in the late 1960s and early 1970s was open, courageous and consistent — and he paid a high price for it.

As a physician in Imotski, he earned the trust of the people, but it was his political energy that marked that period. He was actively involved in the work of Matica hrvatska , wrote articles, gave lectures and encouraged discussions on Croatian identity, economic inequality and political autonomy. He became one of the most recognizable voices of the Croatian national awakening.

In 1969, he was elected a member of the Parliament of the Socialist Republic of Croatia , where from the outset he advocated a fairer position for Croatia within the federation, the development of Dalmatia and the Imotski region, the strengthening of agriculture, health care, education and culture, and opposition to the economic centralism of Belgrade. His speeches were clear, well argued and fearless — and for that reason he very quickly became a target of the Yugoslav services.

In February 1972 , he was accused of “unconstitutional activity” and of links with Croatian intellectuals who advocated greater national rights. His immunity was revoked, he was detained and sentenced to two years and four months in prison , which he served in the notorious Stara Gradiška . He was part of a group of political prisoners whose sacrifice laid the ideological and moral foundations of the future Croatian state.

After his release from prison, he returned to his family and medical profession, but the political wound remained. He died on 3 January 1989, only two years before the beginning of the creation of the state for which he had fought. He did not live to see Croatia, but through his actions he became permanently woven into its foundations.

2.2.3.2. Mijo Grgin (1856.–1933.)

Grgo’s third son, Mijo, married Iva, née Tandara. They had seven children: four sons—Martin (1886–1905), the first Nikola (1889–), Mate (1892–) and the second Nikola (1902–)—and three daughters: Iva (1883–), the first Mara (1886–1891) and the second Mara (1894–). Martin died at the age of nineteen, while the first Nikola and Mate died as young children. No information about the second Nikola has been found apart from the record of his birth. There is no evidence that any of Mijo’s sons married or had children, so this family branch ended.
