It may therefore be assumed that they were no longer alive or that one of them had moved away from Ričice.

THE BEGINNING OF MIGRATION BEYOND RIČICE

In 1819, the family of Martin Tandara, Lovro’s grandfather, was living in Zavelim. Martin’s sons Ivan, Mijo and Grgo were listed there. When the Austrian cadastral survey was conducted in 1835, Martin Tandara, son of the late Petar, was recorded as a property owner in Ričice. At that time, most clans from Ričice owned property in Ričice, Zavelim, Podbila and Vir. These villages belonged to the same parish in Ričice. Many families within the same clan moved from one locality to another, even within Ričice itself. The population was growing rapidly and new living space was needed. In this context, it is understandable that a family, or part of a family, could be recorded in Ričice in one register and in Zavelim in the next, or vice versa. It is known with certainty, however, that Grgo returned with his children from Zavelim to Ričice, where he continued living with his family.

2.2. Martin — son of Petar (★ 1775 – ✝ 1844)

Martin, Petar’s third son, married Ruža, née Lekić. Between the 1806 and 1819 censuses, Martin moved with his family from Ričice to Zavelim together with Ante, the son of his paternal uncle. They had six sons—Ivan, Jure (1811–), Mijo, Grgo, Filip (1821–) and Josip (1824–)—and two daughters, Matija and Ana (1828–). No records of Jure, Filip or Josip have been found apart from their baptismal entries, so it is not known whether they died young or moved away. Mijo’s sons left no male descendants, so the family line was continued only by Martin’s sons Ivan and Grgo.

2.2.3.1.1.3.1. Ante Bruno — son of Mate (★ 1937 – ✝ 1989)

Dr. Bruno Tandara (1937–1989) was the most prominent intellectual from Ričice and an important figure in Croatia’s political awakening in the late 1960s and early 1970s. As a physician, he earned respect for his dedication, expertise and constant availability to those who needed him, but he left his deepest mark through his political activity during the Croatian Spring.

He took an active part in cultural and public life, working with Matica hrvatska and local media. By encouraging public debate, he emphasized the importance of Croatian identity, the development of Dalmatia, a fair distribution of resources within the federation, and the protection of national interests. In 1969 he was elected to the Parliament of the Socialist Republic of Croatia, where he openly advocated decentralization, a stronger Croatian economy, the development of the Imotski region, and equality within the Yugoslav federation of the time.

Because of these views, he became a target of the communist authorities. In a politically motivated trial in 1972, he was sentenced to several years in prison, which he served in the notorious Stara Gradiška prison alongside prominent Croatian political dissidents of the period. His imprisonment and persecution formed part of the broader repression of Croatian intellectuals who sought greater national rights and freedoms.

After his release from prison, he returned to medicine and remained a moral pillar of his community. He died in 1989, shortly before the creation of the Croatian state to which his public activity had made an important contribution. He is remembered as a Croatian patriot, intellectual and political prisoner whose work formed part of the foundations of the political awakening that preceded Croatian independence.
